Built for the big bad.

Three siblings, three houses, and one place they can all reach before the wolf does.

Open on the model and the shadow on the wall. The clients are three siblings who each already have a house and a strong opinion about it. Tonight's question is not whose house is best; it is where all three can go, together, when the shadow arrives. This is a fictional design exercise for the Rikiki example gallery: recommendations are scenario proposals, not structural engineering advice or measured results.

Each sibling protects something different.

 Give each sibling their due before challenging them. Every choice was sensible for the thing that sibling cared about: speed, familiarity, strength. The bottom line of each card is the question that sibling never asked. We are not quoting them; we are describing the gap each point of view leaves.

The wolf does not choose a material. He finds a gap.

Now take the wolf's point of view, on the dark side of the lamp. He does not care which material each sibling prefers. He walks around the building looking for the one place where the boundary is not continuous: an open roof, a soft infill, a way in at ground level. Three good materials can still make one weak house.

They agree to protect one shared refuge first.

Three renovations

Everyone improves their own house and none of them is finished when it matters.

One usable refuge

One complete boundary all three can reach, enter and close.

The trade-off is real: each sibling waits for their own house so that all three are safe sooner.

This is the turning point of the story, so say the cost out loud. Choosing one refuge means each sibling postpones the house they care about. The benefit is that one finished boundary protects all three, instead of three unfinished ones protecting nobody.

"Usable" means all three can reach, enter, close and stay.

A route every sibling can walk in the dark.

A door the smallest sibling can open.

One closure that any of them can operate.

Room for three through a long night.

 The brief needs a definition everyone can check. Four verbs, each owned by the whole family rather than by one sibling. These are requirements for the fictional brief, written so that the acceptance walk at the end can test them one by one.

Straw

Straw keeps the inside warm, once something else keeps the wind out.

Infill inside a closed envelope. Not the outer boundary.
The first sibling's material gets a real job, and it is not the one in the fairytale. Straw is good at comfort and quick to place, so in the refuge it becomes insulation behind the wall. Whether that detail works is for the designer to confirm; this is a proposal, not a rating.

Timber

Timber frames the roof, if every joint is designed, not improvised.

Roof frame the family can maintain. Connections left to be worked out on site.
The second sibling's timber is the right material for a roof they can repair themselves. The weak point is never the timber, it is the joint. Those connections have to be drawn and checked before anyone cuts wood, which is why they appear again on the priority slide.

Brick

Brick makes the shell. A wall with holes is still a wall with holes.

Outer shell around the shared room. Windows, door and flue still open.
The third sibling was closest to right, and that is exactly why the gap is easy to miss. Brick is a good shell. But a shell is only as good as its openings, and every window, the door and the flue are holes the wolf can use. No material here is claimed to be wolf-proof.

The boundary closes only where roof and wall meet.

The three materials meet at three junctions, and each junction belongs to two siblings at once. That is where the design work goes: where the roof bears on the wall, the ridge and frame joints, and the wall head under the eaves. We mark them; the designer details them.

The way in is part of the defence plan.

Approach Entrance Closure Shared room A conceptual route for the brief, not a construction drawing. Each sibling built a house and assumed arrival was obvious. The refuge treats the route as part of the protection: a short approach, an entrance wide enough for three at once, a closure one person can work from inside, and a room set away from the openings.

Survey and design come before any finishing touch.

Confirm the site, the route and what all three need. Roof on wall, frame joints, openings and closure. A continuous boundary with a working door. Decoration waits until everyone can get inside. No costs and no dates here, because none exist before the survey. What we can fix tonight is the order: understand the site, design the junctions, enclose, and only then finish. Anything decorative that appears earlier is a sign the order has slipped.

Each stage ends with something the siblings can check.

A sequence of dependencies, not a schedule. Each stage produces a thing the siblings can hold or walk through, so nobody has to trust a progress report. Step through the timeline one stage at a time. Dates will come from the designer after the survey.
